    What worked
    • Networking and community are the dominant value driver across teams.
    • Bootcamp 1 and the Pitch Prep Workshop are the highest-rated format elements. Use them as the template and don't let revamp energy dilute them.
    • Pre-recorded theory. Teams watched at their own pace and brought questions to Bootcamps and Office Hours.
    • Unplugged Talks at company offices, about an hour, heavy on Q&A, with speakers who talked about what went wrong.
    • Organic outcomes: investor inbound and ongoing mentor relationships for several teams.
    Where it strained
    • Hardware teams are underserved. The mentor pool is software-biased.
    • Mentor advice contradicts itself, with no mechanism for teams to reconcile it.
    • Compressed scheduling. Hackathon, Bootcamp 1 and Bootcamp 2 on three consecutive weekends; one week was not enough to implement feedback.
    • Travel burden falls disproportionately on non-Bucharest teams.
    • Format breaks. Online semifinals after in-person stages; the hybrid Unplugged format left online participants out.
    • Deliverables weren't consistently evaluated. At least one team was promised a review and never got feedback.
    Coachability

    Some participants were hard to coach.

    Some participants were notably arrogant and hard to coach, creating real friction. Teams also don't know how to analyse feedback: what to keep, what to ignore, what to act on. The interventions tried so far haven't clearly worked, which argues for changing the mechanism.

    This comes from direct observation by the team, not from the interview transcripts.

    The evidence base

    Offloading has a cost. Thinking first is the bet.

    Founders are offloading the thinking step to AI, not only the writing step: interviews, competitive analysis, pitch narrative. The output gets more polished while less of their own reasoning sits underneath it.

    The core of the case

    AI offloading has a measurable cost, it lands harder on younger users, and the antidote is doing your own thinking first and using the model as a challenger.

    The evidence is recent and partly correlational, which is why the program does not rest on it alone. It also measures the effect on its own teams.

    Tier A

    The problem, in the population we serve

    Ordered by how directly the evidence bears on adult founders in their mid-20s, and how much weight it can carry if challenged.

    Your Brain on ChatGPT, MIT Media Lab (2025)[2]

    Adults · AIPreprint

    54 participants wrote essays over four months with EEG. Connectivity scaled down as support went up: the ChatGPT group was up to roughly 55% below brain-only. 83% of ChatGPT users could not quote a sentence from the essay they had just written, and essays clustered together within each topic. The authors call it cognitive debt.

    The team had access to the preprinted, under-review version of the study.

    Gerlich, cognitive offloading and critical thinking (2025)[3]

    Adults · AICorrelational

    666 participants of different ages. A significant negative correlation between frequent AI-tool use and critical-thinking ability, mediated by cognitive offloading. Younger participants showed higher dependence and lower critical-thinking scores.

    The Google effect (2011)[4]

    Mechanism

    People forget information they expect to look up again, and remember where it is better than the thing itself. The plausible extension is that AI chat tools push this from facts to reasoning.

    The extension to reasoning is not yet demonstrated. The 2011 finding is used as a mechanism, not as recently reinforced evidence.

    Why some founders' AI output is worse[5][6]

    Teachable skillPractitioner

    Practitioner work on context engineering converges on one point: prompt wording matters far less than the quality and specificity of the material fed in. Feeding AI real source material and interrogating its output is exactly what the program wants to teach.

    Practitioner literature, not peer-reviewed research.

    Tier B

    Why the risk concentrates in this cohort

    This context explains why the group is exposed. It does not prove the interventions work.

    Haidt, The Anxious Generation (2024)[7]

    FramingNarrative

    Argues four harms (social deprivation, sleep disruption, attention fragmentation, addiction) track the smartphone era and concentrate in people who were adolescents during the transition. A threshold cited in Congressional testimony: 1 to 2 hours a day of social media shows no measurable harm, 3 to 4 hours does.

    A book with a strong narrative rather than proof. Useful for framing, not as a causal claim.

    Sweden's reversal (2023)[8]

    Institutional signalContested

    After two decades of digitising classrooms and a decline in fourth-grade reading between the 2016 and 2021 assessments, Sweden funded printed textbooks (about 685 million kronor) and moved to keep devices largely away from under-sixes.

    A national policy reversal, not a controlled experiment. The causal link to the reading decline is the government's read and is contested.

    Screen time and attention[9]

    Children 3–11Extrapolated

    A longitudinal study of just over a thousand children linked screen use to weaker sustained attention and reading to stronger attention, with print tied to better comprehension than digital.

    Applying the same mechanism to people in their mid-20s is the team's extrapolation.

    The counterweight: school phone bans[10]

    ReviewWeak evidence

    Across roughly two dozen studies in a dozen countries, a review judged the overall evidence weak and inconclusive, and found bans produced a short-term spike in disciplinary incidents in year one. The case for device discipline is real but not one-sided.

    Tier C

    The countermeasure: why analog-first is a reasonable bet

    Read as mechanism and precedent, not as proof that these methods improve startup outcomes.

    Handwriting versus typing[11][12]

    StudentsMixed replication

    Laptop note-takers did worse on conceptual questions than longhand note-takers, apparently because handwriting forces paraphrase and synthesis. The closest analog evidence, since it used students.

    A 2019 replication and later work found the effect does not hold consistently. The mechanism is treated as plausible, not settled.

    Embodied cognition[13]

    MechanismYoung children

    In pre-literate children, the reading circuit activates during letter perception only after handwriting practice, not after typing or tracing.

    Robust, but about young children learning letters. Cited as a mechanism for why self-produced work is encoded differently.

    Montessori, Waldorf, Reggio Emilia[14–18]

    Design inspiration

    Concrete before abstract; authoring your own lesson books; the room and visible documentation as a "third teacher" that makes a group's thinking legible.

    Design inspiration for the analog exercises, not outcome evidence for adults. Keep that distinction explicit wherever this is used as rationale.

    Design Sprint and Socratic seminar[19–21]

    Adult practice

    The GV Design Sprint starts with individual paper sketching (Crazy 8s) and is already named in the Module 2 deck. The Socratic seminar maps onto pitch Q&A and mock-interview debriefs, and makes "don't read your slides" structurally impossible.

    What this does not show

    A reasoned bet, settled on our own teams.

    None of this proves that paper-first exercises make founders build better companies. The strongest findings are about children and transfer weakly; the adult and AI findings are recent and partly correlational; the chain from "handwriting aids encoding" to "better pitch, better product" is unvalidated.

    So the claim is stated as a bet: grounded in a plausible mechanism, an adult professional precedent already in the curriculum, and direct observation of this cohort's fluent-but-shallow output.

    The hypothesis we test

    Founders who think first and use AI as a challenger produce better, more defensible work and own more of their reasoning than those who let AI write version one.

    Measured by the share of teams whose deliverable quality improves between Bootcamp 1 and Bootcamp 2, and by the AI-use log.

    Curriculum

    Same blocks, new order. Paper, peers, then AI.

    Theory stays pre-recorded so in-person time goes to feedback and mentoring. The analog sequence moves into existing blocks rather than adding new time.

    The learning sequence

    1. Think in analog

      Write, sketch, color-code and map assumptions on paper. Force slow thinking before any external assistance.

    2. Think together

      Present unfinished work to peers, take real human questions and defend the reasoning out loud.

    3. Challenge with AI

      Only after an original version exists. AI challenges assumptions, finds blind spots and argues the other side.

    4. Human synthesis

      The founder makes the final call. The output stays human-authored and the founder owns the reasoning.

    AI is

    A challenger, a critic, a devil's advocate, a reviewer, a stress tester, a debate partner, a perspective generator.

    AI is not

    The founder, the product manager, the storyteller, the strategist.

    One framing for Orientation: AI doesn't accelerate anything if you don't already know in detail what you want.

    Ten more analog exercises to choose from+
    • Competitor war wall (Bootcamp 1): printed competitor one-pagers sorted into direct, indirect and ecosystem columns, with a paper changelog of moves.
    • Card-sort feature prioritization: one feature per index card on an effort-versus-impact grid.
    • Paper prototyping station (Module 2): testers tap paper screens and a teammate swaps in the next screen by hand.
    • Journey mapping on butcher paper (Bootcamp 2): customer and user journeys drawn by hand, walked through standing up.
    • Pivot decision card (Bootcamp 2): validation evidence on cards, sorted against the printed trigger and anti-trigger list with a mentor present.
    • Hardware makerspace hour: breadboards, cardboard and form-factor mockups with dedicated hardware mentors. Also a direct fix for the hardware-mentor gap.
    • Silent brainstorm: everyone writes alone first, so the loudest voice doesn't anchor the room.
    • Elevator pitch swap: another team pitches your one-line card back live.
    • Red-flag bell round: peers ring a bell the moment they hear an item from the red-flags list.
    • Analog round at Semifinals or Demo Day: printed boards or props instead of slides, even if judges join remotely.

    Which exercises to pilot first, and in which bootcamp, is still to be decided.

    Worked example: building a pitch+

    Today many founders ask AI to write the pitch first. The new sequence:

    1. Write the pitch yourself.
    2. Present it.
    3. Get feedback from other founders.
    4. Improve it yourself.
    5. Use AI to critique it.
    6. Let AI challenge the narrative, find the weaknesses and ask the hard questions.
    7. Rewrite the pitch yourself.

    What changes in the program blocks

    Orientation · first Monday after the Hackathon
    • How to be a mentee.
    • VC and partner open validation.
    • The program's AI position, stated once: not banned, not a ghostwriter, used as a challenger.
    • The integrity line: AI-fabricated interviews and competitors don't count. Real people and real sources, or the deliverable is invalid.
    Back to Fundamentals · moved earlier

    Ran online after the 2026 semifinals. For 2027 it moves before Bootcamp 2, so it lands ahead of the brutal-feedback block and addresses coachability while it can still change something.

    Bootcamp 1 · AI literacy and curation track
    • Master prompt per startup that challenges ideas, requires sources and lists assumptions.
    • Deliverable ranking of three anonymised 2026 deliverables before teams write their own.
    • Fallacy identification, optionally one a day through the app or WhatsApp group.
    • Competitive analysis exam: every data point sourced, 30 minutes live analysis, then feedback.
    • Pitch deck cross-review and simple templates provided upfront.
    Bootcamp 1 · customer discovery track
    • Teams submit 5 hypotheses plus the experiments to validate them.
    • Interview guides prepared and sent in advance.
    • Two or three guides selected for live mock interviews in front of an audience.
    • How competitive analysis feeds customer discovery, taught explicitly.
    Bootcamp 2 · expectations and a pivot session
    • Stated in advance: customer discovery motion and lessons, a complete competitive analysis and the decisions it drove, an MVP user journey with its hypothesis.
    • Before: office hours and online mentoring. During: a working session to internalise feedback and pivot, with coach support. After: an at-risk shortlist; the cut lands a week later.
    Sessions currently missing
    • Outreach and communication working session with alumni, including mock cold calling, which worked when run with LilPath.
    • Tech-focused session on building for current needs, ideally by the team's own technical mentor.
    • GTM and user acquisition, earlier. Building is now the easy part; distribution isn't.
    • Themed pitches on a specific theme or on progress since the last one.
    Unplugged additions and fixes+
    • Standing deliverable template: what we planned, what we did, what surprised us, what we changed, what we asked AI, what we verified. This is the AI-use log, already in usable shape.
    • Alumni session on getting your first pitch or first client, in B2B and B2C versions.
    • Fix the hybrid format: commit to a format that works remotely, or stop treating the online audience as participating.
    • Shorten alumni panels or run them in person. Two hours online felt distant.
    • Speaker vetting for AI stance, or the program contradicts itself from its own stage. The vetting standard is still to be defined.

    How mentors plug in

    Mentors co-author the rubric each skill enforces. One shared, mentor-authored standard checked across teams is the actual fix for contradictory mentor advice. Mentor sign-up forms add a field for AI and skills-tooling competency, as a routing signal.

    Risk named up front

    Founders can learn to satisfy the rubric mechanically instead of reflecting. Output stays a discussion prompt for the mentor session, never a pass/fail gate, and prompts vary so they can't be pattern-matched.

    Proposed course

    How AI Tricks You

    A standalone workshop or a new block inside Module 1, in the same block-and-deliverable format as the existing decks.

    1. How AI flatters you. Fluency bias; most pitch red flags are what AI-flattened writing looks like.
    2. What you lose when you offload. The research above, taught plainly.
    3. How AI fails silently here. Fabricated competitors, synthetic transcripts, idea homogenization, AI as an invisible third mentor.
    4. Context engineering, the actual skill. Context Poisoning, Distraction, Confusion and Clash; the Generator, Reflector, Curator loop.
    5. Practical exercise. Teams red-team their own real deliverable live, mentor-supervised.

    Deliverable: an AI-use log per team stating what was AI-assisted versus self-generated, which also operationalises the integrity rule.

    AI risks the design exists to prevent

    Fabricated research

    A plausible competitor list or a synthetic interview transcript that never happened. Needs a blunt integrity rule: transcripts traceable to a real person, competitor claims sourced.

    The integrity rule itself is still to be drafted.

    Fluency bias and homogenization

    Polished output reads as more competent than it is, and similar prompts converge on similar business models, undercutting Blue Ocean and ERRC.

    Silent mentor and data leakage

    AI adds a third, invisible voice to contradictory advice. Interview transcripts pasted into consumer tools are a GDPR problem for an EU-based program, so a plain data-handling rule is needed.

    Mentor retro
    The problems
    • Mentors aren't matched well to teams, so some feedback is noise.
    • Some mentors lack context and communication skills.
    • The pool skews corporate; more founders are needed in the room.
    • Science, neurodivergence and agriculture need dedicated domain mentors.
    • Deliverables aren't consistently evaluated.
    • Teams admitted on potential rather than idea strength don't post enough.
    New structure
    • Hands-on mentors, 2 to 3 per team, one business and one tech. One mentor can cover multiple teams.
    • PODs: 3 to 5 teams, one facilitator (core team or trusted coach), 2 to 3 mentors, 45 minutes to an hour weekly, for the whole program.
    • A vetted mentor core, admitted after a 30-minute conversation with the core team.
    • Mentor supply mapping from the last two years, so 2027 knows which industries it can serve before promising them.
    • Hands-on mentors evaluate deliverables, not just advise.
    • A control mechanism for mismatch, so a bad pairing is fixable rather than endured.
    • Mentors register interest in a specific team during the Hackathon. A kickoff for hands-on mentors and coaches before Bootcamp 1.
    • Hands-on alumni, if validated: start small with warm intros per team and see what happens naturally.
    Accountability loop
    • Weekly coach checkpoint plus a form the core team can see.
    • Weekly public status check in front of other teams.
    • Coach journal, promoted more actively.
    • Best-practice sharing between coaches.
    Mentor tooling
    • Teach mentors to evaluate LLM output against a shared standard.
    • High-level check prompts as a triage pass.
    • A common slide guideline, suggested by a participant after contradictory advice across two Pitch Prep sessions.
    Supporting mechanisms
    • Mock interview matching through the IL community, at least one guaranteed interview per team.
    • Community members matched by industry or department.
    • For unusual teams, mentor search starts at the Hackathon.
    • A workshop on getting the most out of mentoring.
    Coaching response to low coachability
    Decision

    Keep the brutal feedback format public and unsoftened. Add private 1:1 coaching alongside it.

    Group feedback stays for skill correction and culture; private coaching covers the interpersonal and self-awareness work a public format can't do.

    Feasibility depends on cohort size. Coaching logistics (who coaches, hours per team) are still to be worked out and turn on only once the cohort drops to about 25.

    • Make coachability a selection criterion. Arrogance and openness to feedback are visible at the hackathon and validation-bootcamp stage if anyone is looking.
    • Reuse the mechanisms already proposed. Live Socratic Q&A, the red-flag bell round and unscripted pitch defense expose overconfidence faster than a mentor saying so.
    • Name it in existing culture moments. Bootcamp 2 already opens with "brutal honest feedback, ownership, no BS"; make coachability an explicit stated value there.
    • Address it from the side. Teams that can judge quality in someone else's work hear judgment of their own better.

    Safeguarding is handled separately from the curriculum: a juror code of conduct and a reporting channel, dealt with quietly. Both are still to be drafted.

    Cohort sizing

    Cut after Bootcamp 2, plus one week

    The cut lands one week after Bootcamp 2, not at its closing bell. Teams said one week between bootcamps wasn't enough to act on feedback; the buffer measures whether a team applied feedback, not where it stood on the day.

    IL Beyond

    Replace the cliff with a slope.

    Reaching Demo Day was never the same as being fundable. Coaching, connections and accountability stop right at graduation, when teams need them most. IL Beyond carries them forward.

    Why it exists

    Many teams graduate with a good demo and a real story but aren't investable in any way a VC would recognize: no incorporation, no cap table, no repeatable sales motion, sometimes no stable founding team. Most stop building the week the program ends. Many don't know IL is still willing to help, or don't ask.

    Who's eligible: this year's graduates and alumni from any past year. A 2023 or 2024 team still building is a legitimate candidate. Selection reads the team's running documentation from the whole funnel instead of reconstructing months of signal from one interview.

    Objectives
    • At least 2 companies finish investment-ready for a EUR 300k to 500k pre-seed round, meaning they sign a term sheet.
    • At least 1 company reaches an actual investment process with EGV or another pre-seed institutional investor, meaning the money lands.

    PMF and a technical moat. The IL MVP becomes a product real external users interact with.

    Deliverables and measures+
    • Updated pitch deck with PMF evidence.
    • One-page technical roadmap naming the moat and the 100x scaling risk.
    • Log of active users or customers with evidence.
    • Written core-team commitment.

    Deliverables are drafted and still to be confirmed. Losing a co-founder is not automatically disqualifying; the program adds co-founder matching or mediation instead of a penalty.

    S2 · Sell · October to December
    3–4 teams

    Incorporate, sign commercial contracts, prepare the round. One or two teams should exit with a term sheet.

    Deliverables and evidence+
    • Incorporation documents, founder agreement, clean cap table.
    • At least one signed contract, LOI or purchase order.
    • Investor deck, draft data room, financial model with key assumptions validated.
    • Evidence by type: B2B SaaS, enterprise and deep tech, consumer, hardware.

    Deliverables are drafted and still to be confirmed.

    S3 · Raise · January to February
    2 teams

    From term sheet to money in the bank, with angels and VCs brought in as co-investors.

    How the raise works+
    • EGV leads or co-leads where relevant, with co-investors alongside.
    • Angels and pre-seed VCs invited by sector and check size.
    • A 48-hour warm intro window after any pitch or investor meeting.
    • Angel access before the round is fully priced.
    • Pitch at existing meetups such as Tech Angels and Bravva instead of only an IL-owned room.

    Next step: validate the model with EGV's co-investors and independent Romanian angels before building it out.

    Moves to IL Beyond

    The EGV Investment Prize

    A EUR 500,000 ticket offered on Demo Day night to a team that is usually not incorporated was stage-mismatched, and led to the prize not being awarded on stage in 2026. In 2027, EGV's capital is deployed at the end of Stage 3 into a team with a signed term sheet, alongside IL's own EUR 20,000 angel bridge.

    Demo Day keeps the recognition awards: Grand Prize, Best Emerging Start-up, Best Product, Public Vote.

    This requires EGV's agreement to reposition the prize, and clear communication to teams that the path to EGV capital now runs through IL Beyond.

    Investment ready, defined

    Commercially
    • Real users, design partners or customers.
    • Validation appropriate to the business model.
    • A credible route to a scalable business, evidenced through a validated adoption channel or acquisition experiment.

    The scalability bar should be calibrated against how EGV and partner VCs judge it in practice; at pre-seed they mostly eyeball it.

    Tech
    • Functioning product or technically validated prototype.
    • Credible technical differentiation.
    • A plan to scale: what breaks at 100x.
    Organization
    • Stable, committed founding team.
    • Incorporated company, clean founder equity, IP owned by the company.
    • Clear use of the EUR 300k to 500k round.
    • Investment deck and a minimal data room.
    Selection for Stage 1
    • At least 2 full-time founders and 1 technical founder.
    • Technical know-how, data advantage, IP or defensible architecture.
    • Venture-scale potential and a reason 300k to 500k would accelerate the company.
    • Coachability, reassessed fresh, informed by the team's documentation but not carried over as pass/fail.

    Process: a 1-hour interview plus a 1-hour coaching and feedback session with the core team and selected jury members.

    Tracking and lead coach
    • Weekly founder email: commitments, results, key KPI, learning, blocker.
    • A 30-minute lead-coach check-in every 2 weeks; a monthly board-style review; a monthly founder dinner.
    • Verification, not just trust: evidence behind any load-bearing KPI.
    • The lead coach is a more senior, selectively recruited role than a core-program coach.

    Open in the notes: whether the lead coach gets an option on a slice of IL's allocation, and who owns the tech and product reviews.

    Challenger skills, extended
    • red-team-my-pitch runs against the investor deck unchanged.
    • juror-simulator becomes investor Q&A rehearsal.
    • data-room-auditor (new): unsupported claims, missing source documents, numbers that don't match between deck and model.
    • cap-table-sanity-check (new): equity that doesn't reflect contribution, missing IP assignment, unclear vesting.

    The AI-use log extends to Stage 2 and 3 deliverables: every number and claim in the deck and data room checked before either goes out.

    Program-level KPIs

    North star: the share of accelerator companies that raise institutional or angel pre-seed capital, or reach equivalent self-sustaining traction, within 12 months.

    Supporting: progression between stages, incorporation, active users, paid customers or pilots, term sheets, capital raised at 3, 6 and 12 months, survival at 12 months, acceptance into strong external accelerators.

    The KPI list is an early draft and needs a second pass.

    How other programs handle this gap+
    • Techstars[22], the closest direct comparison: a three-month cohort, USD 220,000 investment, lifetime network access. Alumni average a first post-program raise above USD 1M.
    • EIC Pre-Accelerator[23], closest in stated purpose: lump-sum grants (EUR 300k to 500k in 2025, rising to EUR 500k to 1M in 2027) plus coaching and pitching bootcamps.
    • Mountside Ventures[24]: free investor-readiness programmes for companies raising in 3 to 12 months, with templates and a data room checklist.
    • Alchemist Accelerator[25], closest on cohort size and coaching: about 25 teams per class, every founder paired with an experienced coach.
    • On demo days: in a market with as few pre-seed investors as Romania, the constraint is reaching the investors who exist, which argues for showing up where they already gather.

    Still to discuss: whether IL should help or push teams to apply to YC, Techstars or Seedcamp.
