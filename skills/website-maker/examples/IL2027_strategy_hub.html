The evidence

What the research says about AI and thinking

Our founders hand AI more than the writing. They hand it the interviews, the competitive analysis and the pitch narrative, and the output looks polished with less of their own reasoning underneath. The most direct evidence that this costs something is recent, and it is about adults.

MIT Media Lab, Your Brain on ChatGPT (2025)

54 people wrote essays over four months while EEG measured their brain activity. One group used ChatGPT, one a search engine, one nothing. Brain connectivity fell as outside support rose. The ChatGPT group had the weakest, least distributed networks, up to about 55% below the unaided group. 83% of ChatGPT users could not quote a sentence from the essay they had just written, and their essays were statistically alike within each topic. The authors call the build-up cognitive debt. We read the preprint, which is still under review. [1]

Gerlich, 2025

666 people of different ages. Frequent AI-tool use went with lower critical thinking, and cognitive offloading explained the link. Younger participants showed more dependence and lower critical-thinking scores than older ones. [2]

The Google effect

People forget what they are confident they can look up, and remember where to find it instead. The internet works as outside memory. The plausible next step, which has not been shown, is that AI chat tools move this from facts to reasoning, so you stop retaining how you reached an answer. The original finding is from 2011, so we use it as a mechanism. [3]

Why some founders get better AI output

Practitioner work on context engineering agrees on one point: prompt wording matters far less than the quality and specificity of the material and domain knowledge you feed in. Output follows how much real evidence, correction and iteration the founder brings. This is practitioner writing and not peer-reviewed research, and it is the part that turns into a teachable skill. [4][5]

Taken together, AI offloading has a measurable cost, it lands harder on younger users, and the antidote is doing your own thinking first and using the model as a challenger. The evidence is recent and partly correlational, which is why the program does not rest on it alone. It also measures the effect on its own teams.
Context

Screens, attention and reading

This is background. It does not prove that our interventions work.

Haidt, The Anxious Generation (2024)

Argues that four harms (social deprivation, sleep disruption, attention fragmentation and addiction) track the smartphone era. A threshold cited in Congressional testimony: 1 to 2 hours a day of social media shows no measurable harm, 3 to 4 hours does. It is a book with a strong narrative, so we use it for framing. [6]

Sweden reverses course

After about two decades of digitising classrooms, Swedish fourth-grade reading proficiency fell between the 2016 and 2021 assessments. In 2023 the government funded printed textbooks (about 685 million kronor) and moved to keep devices largely away from under-sixes. It is a national policy reversal and not a controlled experiment, and the link to the decline is the government's own read and contested. [7]

Screen time and attention

A study of just over a thousand children aged 3 to 11 linked screen use to weaker sustained attention and reading to stronger attention, with print reading tied to better comprehension than digital. We extrapolate that the same mechanism applies to adults. [8]

The counterweight: school phone bans

A review of roughly two dozen studies in a dozen countries judged the overall evidence weak and inconclusive, and found a short-term rise in disciplinary incidents in year one. The case for device discipline is real and has two sides, and saying so keeps the rest credible. [9]

The countermeasure

Why paper-first is a reasonable bet

If the risk is that founders skip their own reasoning, the countermeasure is to make original thinking happen before any model is opened. We read this research as mechanism and precedent, and as no proof that it improves startup outcomes.

Handwriting versus typing

In several studies, laptop note-takers did worse on conceptual questions than longhand note-takers, apparently because laptops invite verbatim copying while handwriting forces paraphrase. It used students, so it is the closest evidence to our setting. A 2019 replication and later work found the effect does not hold up consistently, so we treat the mechanism as plausible and unsettled. [10][11]

Embodied cognition

In pre-literate children, the brain's reading circuit activates during letter perception only after handwriting practice, not after typing or tracing. The finding is robust but about young children, so we cite it as a reason physical, self-produced work may be encoded differently. [12]

Teaching precedents

Montessori has children handle a concrete object until a relationship is internalised, then move to the abstract symbol. Waldorf has students hand-write and illustrate their own lesson books. Reggio Emilia treats the physical environment and visible documentation as a third teacher. These inspire the analog exercises and are no outcome evidence for adults. [13][14][15][16][17]

Methods adults already use

The Google Ventures Design Sprint starts with individual paper sketching (Crazy 8s) before any group or digital work, and our Module 2 deck already names it. A Socratic seminar is live and unscripted, with no notes and no slides. It maps onto pitch Q&A and mock-interview debriefs, and it makes reading your slides structurally impossible. [18][19][20]

The limits

What this evidence does not show

None of it proves that paper-first exercises make founders build better companies. The strongest findings are about children and transfer weakly. The most relevant findings, on adults and AI, are recent and partly correlational. The chain from handwriting aiding encoding to a better pitch and a better product is unvalidated.

That is a reason to describe the approach accurately and keep it. Analog first, with AI as a challenger, is a reasoned bet. It rests on a plausible mechanism, an adult professional precedent already in our curriculum, and our own observation of fluent but shallow work in pitches and interviews. Unlike a published study, we can settle it on our own teams.

The 2027 deliverable-quality measures are the experiment: the share of teams whose deliverable quality improves between Bootcamp 1 and Bootcamp 2, and the AI-use log. If the mechanism is real, it shows up there.
The hypothesis

What we act on, and what we test

Offloading has a cost

The credible countermeasure is to force original thinking into existence before the model is opened. The analog-first sequence and the curriculum additions put "build it yourself first" into specific existing blocks.

Challenger skills are teachable

The challenger-skills tooling and the "How AI Tricks You" course turn "use AI as an adversary" into something founders can learn.

The failure modes have names

The AI-risk register names fabricated research, fluency bias and idea homogenization, which is what this design exists to prevent.

The same logic runs through the parts of the program that are not about AI. Feedback is where thinking gets stress-tested. The mentor and coach redesign, the feedback culture and the focused cohort all follow from wanting fewer, better-supported teams who can absorb hard feedback and not hand it to a model.

The hypothesis: founders who think first and use AI as a challenger produce better, more defensible work and own more of their reasoning than those who let AI write version one. The Bootcamp 1 to Bootcamp 2 measures are how we find out.
The 2026 cohort

What 2026 told us

Interviews with the 2026 cohort, plus observation and the mentor retro. The findings are the improvement targets for a program that is fundamentally sound.

What worked

  • Networking and community were the main value for teams.
  • Bootcamp 1 and the Pitch Prep Workshop were the highest-rated parts.
  • Pre-recorded theory worked. Teams watched at their own pace, paused, took notes, and brought questions to bootcamps and Office Hours.
  • Unplugged Talks worked at real company offices, kept to about an hour, heavy on Q&A, with speakers who talked about what went wrong as well as what went right.
  • Several teams report organic outcomes after the program: investor inbound and ongoing mentor relationships.
  • A mentor spent two hours teaching a team to build their first website and paid for their first month of subscription. One introverted team member visibly opened up over the program.

What requests a fix

  • Hardware teams were underserved, because the mentor pool is software-biased.
  • Mentor advice contradicted itself, with no way for teams to reconcile it.
  • Hackathon, Bootcamp 1 and Bootcamp 2 ran on three consecutive weekends. One participant missed Bootcamp 1 because of the pileup, and a team needed a day or two just to agree on a direction after conflicting feedback.
  • Travel fell harder on teams outside Bucharest.
  • The online semifinal broke continuity with the in-person stages, and participants found it backwards that Boost Day was physical while the semifinals were online.
  • Deliverables were not consistently evaluated. At least one team uploaded theirs, was told they would be reviewed, and got no feedback.
  • The hybrid Unplugged format left online participants out: audio needed constant concentration, chat went unread, and one participant waited 35 minutes to be called on.
The risks

AI risks worth watching

Fabricated research

Competitive analysis requires real sources and discovery requires 10+ real interviews. AI can confidently produce a plausible competitor list or an interview transcript that never happened, and hit the deliverable count with fiction. The fix is a blunt integrity rule: transcripts traceable to a real person, competitor claims sourced.

Fluency bias

AI-polished output reads as more competent than it is. Mentors and jurors risk scoring polish over substance, which is what the pitch red-flags list already warns against.

Idea homogenization

Many teams prompting similar models with similar framing risk convergent business models and pitch language, which undercuts the point of Blue Ocean thinking and ERRC.

AI as a silent extra mentor

Founders get advice from AI on top of human mentor advice. That adds a third, invisible voice to the contradictory-advice problem, from a source nobody can see or moderate.

Data leakage and GDPR

Interview transcripts contain real people's personal data. Founders pasting them into consumer AI tools is a compliance problem for an EU-based program, and it needs a plain data-handling rule.

Curriculum

How the program teaches, and what changes.

The analog-first sequence, the exercises that carry it, the challenger skills, and what is new in each module for 2027.

The sequence

Analog first, AI second

Human thinking first, AI amplification second. The goal is that founders build their own thinking before AI amplifies it. Four stages, in order, applied to existing blocks and added as no new time.

1. Think in analog

Founders write, sketch, color-code and map assumptions on paper. The aim is slow thinking and original reasoning before any outside help enters.

2. Think together

Ideas get discussed with peers. Teams present unfinished work, take real questions and defend their reasoning out loud. That builds confidence in their own judgment.

3. Challenge with AI

Only after a founder has produced an original version does AI enter. Its job is to challenge assumptions, find blind spots, propose alternatives, play devil's advocate and generate counterarguments.

4. Human synthesis

The founder makes the final call. The output stays human-authored, and the founder owns the reasoning as well as the prompt.

By the end of the program the workflow reads: Observe, Think, Sketch, Discuss, Build, Challenge with AI, Synthesize, Decide. It should no longer read Prompt, Copy, Present.

What AI is, and is not, in this program is set out on the Vision page.

The exercises

Ten analog exercises

These ten are examples. We will choose from them.

Competitor war wall (Bootcamp 1)

A physical wall with printed competitor one-pagers. Teams move cards into direct, indirect and ecosystem columns as a group, and keep a running paper changelog of competitor moves.

Card-sort feature prioritization

Each feature on its own index card, placed physically on an effort-versus-impact grid.

Paper prototyping station (Module 2)

The first prototype is sketched on paper and not built in Figma. Testers tap the paper screens and a teammate swaps in the next screen by hand.

Journey mapping on butcher paper (Bootcamp 2)

A long paper roll taped to a wall. The team draws the customer and user journeys by hand, with sticky notes for touchpoints and pain points, and walks through them standing up.

Pivot decision card (Bootcamp 2)

The team writes its actual validation evidence on index cards, then sorts it against a printed trigger and anti-trigger list with a mentor present. It forces a documented yes or no.

Hardware makerspace hour

Physical prototyping with breadboards, cardboard and form-factor mockups, run by dedicated hardware mentors. It also helps fix the hardware-mentor coverage gap.

Silent brainstorm before group discussion

Everyone writes alone first, then posts to a wall, then the group discusses. It stops the loudest voice anchoring the room.

Elevator pitch swap

Founders write their one-line pitch on an index card and swap with a stranger team, who pitch it back live using only the card.

Red-flag bell round

Live, low-stakes pitch practice in front of peers. Anyone can ring a bell the moment they hear an item from the red-flags list.

Analog round at Semifinals or Demo Day

Even if judges join remotely, run at least one round with printed boards or physical props instead of slides.

The hidden curriculum: by the end of the program, founders should be able to trust their own intuition, reason independently, tolerate uncertainty, build ideas from first principles, defend their arguments, improve through critique and use AI on purpose. The founders who stand out will be the ones whose thinking is valuable enough that AI can amplify it.
Challenger skills

Five tools that make AI an adversary

These five are examples, and we will choose from them. The AI conversation moves from banning AI to teaching founders to use it as an adversary. Each skill follows the same four stages: build it on paper, discuss it with peers, test it against an AI challenger, then bring both to a mentor.

mom-test-interrogator

Flags Mom Test violations in interview questions or a transcript: leading questions, would-you-buy-this hypotheticals and praise-fishing.

red-team-my-pitch

Runs a pitch script against the existing red-flags list, as a first pass before a mentor sees it. It has the most source material already, so it is likely the fastest first build.

blind-spot-competitor-hunt

Searches adversarially for competitors a team did not list, especially indirect and ecosystem ones, which teams under-research.

assumption-stress-test

Checks a hypothesis list for what is falsifiable and tested versus vague padding, which the "10+ hypotheses" requirement invites.

juror-simulator

Generates hard live follow-up questions from the gaps in a pitch. Founders use it to rehearse, and mentors use it to sharpen Bootcamp 2 questions.

How mentors plug in

Mentors co-author the rubric each skill enforces, so there is one shared standard checked across teams. Mentor sign-up forms add a field confirming AI and skills-tooling competency, used to route mentors to sessions that need it.

The risk, named up front: founders can learn to satisfy the rubric mechanically instead of reflecting. The mitigation is to keep the output as a discussion prompt for the mentor session and not a pass or fail gate, and to vary the prompts so they cannot be pattern-matched.

Proposed course

How AI Tricks You

A standalone workshop, or a new block inside Module 1, built in the same format as the existing curriculum decks: block structure and a deliverable at the end.

Block 1: how AI flatters you

Fluency bias, and why polished output reads as more competent than it is. It ties into the pitch red-flags list, since most of those red flags are what AI-flattened writing looks like.

Block 2: what you lose when you offload

The research taught plainly: cognitive debt, the critical-thinking correlation and its age skew, and the Google effect extended from facts to reasoning.

Block 3: how AI fails silently

Fabricated competitors and synthetic interview transcripts that hit the deliverable count without being real. Idea homogenization. AI as an invisible third mentor.

Block 4: context engineering

What using AI well looks like. Teach Context Poisoning, Context Distraction, Context Confusion and Context Clash (the technical name for the mentor-contradiction problem), and the Generator, Reflector, Curator loop: build, critique, refine.

Block 5: practical exercise

Founders red-team their own real deliverable live in the room with the challenger skills, mentor-supervised, with the output treated as a discussion prompt and no grade.

The deliverable

An AI-use log per team: a plain statement of what was AI-assisted and what was self-generated. It also puts the integrity rule into practice.

2026

The curriculum as delivered in 2026

Theory was pre-recorded and grouped into modules so in-person time could go to feedback and mentoring instead of lecture. Participant interviews confirmed it worked.

Module 1: UX and customer discovery

Four blocks: UX versus UI (Norman, Krug, Nielsen-Norman). Customer discovery built on Steve Blank's Four Steps to the Epiphany, with a 5-step process, a hypothesis template and interviewing via the Mom Test. Experiments, with hypothesis-driven testing, Minimum Viable Experiment and named test types (landing page, Wizard of Oz, A/B, fake door, smoke test), plus a light jobs-to-be-done intro. Persona building. Each block pairs with an AI fit and an AI limits slide.

Deliverables: personas, 10+ hypotheses, 10+ interviews, 1+ experiment. Bootcamp prerequisites: the Module 1 workshops plus a 30-second elevator pitch before Bootcamp 1.

Bootcamp 1: competitive analysis
  • Blue Ocean and Red Ocean, with a recommended hybrid approach.
  • Three competitor types: direct, indirect (including do nothing) and ecosystem threats.
  • Discovery sources: Dealroom, Product Hunt, AlternativeTo, G2 and Capterra, Perplexity.
  • Three lenses: positioning, moats, customer feedback and gaps.
  • Structuring method: a snapshot database, deep-dive one-pagers and a competitor-move changelog. Differentiation via ERRC.
  • Deliverable: 5+ competitors researched, 2+ indirect.
Module 2: user testing

Names the process lineage: Design Thinking, Lean, Double Diamond, Customer Development, Design Sprint. The core distinction is user testing versus usability testing. Test types: Think Aloud, lab usability testing, guerrilla and hallway testing, beta testing. Prototype types: static versus interactive, sketch, wireframe, mock-up. Deliverables: wireframes and prototypes of the main flows, tested with 10+ users.

Bootcamp 2: business models and journeys

A value proposition framework resembling the Value Proposition Canvas, tied to the Lean Model Canvas. Business model archetypes with real comparisons: subscription, usage-based, ads-based, marketplace, razor-and-blade, plus AI-era models (IP licensing, data monetisation, API and platform). Freemium versus free trial, and a pilot-program checklist.

Customer journey (multi-channel, long relationship) is separated from user journey (single session, in-product). Bootcamp 2 also frames team culture around honest feedback and teaches a pivoting framework: what pivoting is (switching ICP, changing the problem, adapting tech to a new use case), what it is not (starting over, a knee-jerk reaction), with explicit triggers and anti-triggers.

Workshop: how to make people care

Framed as "pitching is dead in 2026, how to make people care in 2 minutes." A taxonomy of pitch types by why, what and format. A pitch-content checklist: problem, solution, differentiator, GTM, competition, team, traction, ask, business model, timeline. IL formats: IdeaJams, the Hackathon 1-minute pitch, the Boost Day 2-minute pitch, Semifinals and Demo Day. Q&A handling and voice coaching. Red flags: no value clarity in 30 seconds, huge market, no competition, buzzword overdose, reading slides, no clear ask.

What it has to solve

What the 2026 cohort and mentors told us

  • Mentors are not always matched well to teams, so some feedback is noise.
  • Some mentors lack the context and the communication skills to be useful to a team.
  • The pool skews corporate. Fewer mentors have built a startup, and more founders are needed in the room.
  • Some industries need dedicated mentors with real domain experience: science, neurodivergence, agriculture.
  • Deliverables are not consistently evaluated or given feedback on.
The new structure

How mentoring works in 2027

Hands-on mentors

Two to three per team, one business and one tech, with one mentor able to cover several teams. This directly addresses the tech-side gap. Hands-on mentors evaluate deliverables and do more than advise.

PODs

Three to five teams with one facilitator and two to three mentors, held for the whole program. The facilitator is the core team or a trusted coach. Sessions run 45 minutes to an hour each week.

A vetted mentor core

Mentors are admitted after a 30-minute conversation with the core team and matched by need and not by availability.

Mentor supply mapping

Extract the industry and business-or-tech split from the last two years of mentors, so 2027 knows which industries it can serve before promising them.

A kickoff meeting

For hands-on mentors and coaches before Bootcamp 1. It is framed as exclusive and doubles as networking.

A fix for bad pairings

A control mechanism for mentor-team mismatch, so a pairing that is not working can be fixed.

Mentors raise their hand

Mentors register interest in going hands-on with a specific team during the Hackathon, before selection is finalised.

Hands-on alumni

If validated: test whether non-founder alumni would help a junior team. The first iteration is small: find relevant alumni per team, make a warm intro, and see whether anything happens naturally.

One shared rubric

A small group of top mentors co-authors one rubric, so teams stop hearing six contradictory opinions on the same slide. Mentor sign-up forms confirm who is trained to evaluate AI-assisted work.

Accountability

Staying close to the teams

The accountability loop

  • A weekly checkpoint with the coach plus a form: what they did, what they discovered, what they changed, what is next. The core team can see the form.
  • A weekly public status check, with interviews run and progress, in front of the other teams. It doubles as team bonding.
  • A coach journal, promoted more actively than in 2026.
  • A best-practice sharing loop between coaches.

Mentor tooling

  • Teach mentors to evaluate LLM output, with a shared standard for what good looks like, so feedback on AI-assisted work is consistent.
  • High-level check prompts that mentors can run on a deliverable before reading it closely, as a first pass.
  • A common slide guideline, suggested by a participant after getting contradictory slide advice across two Pitch Prep sessions.
Matching

Connecting teams to the right people

Mock interview matching

Teams describe the kind of person they need and the request goes to the IL community. Every team gets at least one guaranteed interview. It matters most for B2B and is easier for B2C. It gets prototyped at the recruitment bootcamp.

Community matching

Match IL community members to teams by team description, industry or department.

Unusual teams

For exotic teams, start looking for the right mentor from the Hackathon, before they are in the program.

Using mentoring well

A workshop on how to get the most out of mentoring, plus structured mentoring sessions in place of free-form ones.

Feedback culture

Honest feedback, with room to take it in

Bootcamp 2 already opens with a "brutal honest feedback, ownership, no BS" framing. The 2027 plan keeps it and adds to it.

  • Keep the brutal feedback format as it is, public and unsoftened, for skill correction and culture.
  • Add private 1:1 coaching alongside it, for the interpersonal and self-awareness work a public format cannot do. Feasibility depends on cohort size, and a focused cohort makes it practical.
  • Make openness to feedback an explicit, stated value at Bootcamp 2, and look for it early, at the hackathon and the validation bootcamp.
  • Live Socratic Q&A, the red-flag bell round and unscripted pitch defense show reasoning live, which tells everyone more than polish does.
  • The mentee orientation and the deliverable-ranking exercise help from the side: teams that can judge quality in someone else's work are better at hearing judgment of their own.
Year ahead

Cohort and recruiting.

A focused cohort, the numbers behind it, how we recruit, the Hackathon, and the measures we track.

The cohort

A focused cohort, set one week after Bootcamp 2

Intake already halved between 2025 and 2026, from 80 teams to 41, and Demo Day fielded 17 teams in both years. Half the intake produced the same number of finalists.

Why one extra week

Teams in 2026 reported that one week between bootcamps was not enough to act on feedback, especially when the feedback was contradictory and the team needed a day or two just to agree on a direction. Setting the cohort a week after Bootcamp 2 means it reflects whether a team applied the feedback, and not where it happened to stand on the day.

More attention per team

Every team got about 53 hours of mentoring in 2026. At about 25 teams instead of 41, that number goes up. It is also what makes private 1:1 coaching practical.

Clear evidence behind it

Bootcamp 1 and the analog exercises already produce concrete, comparable artifacts per team: interview evidence, hypothesis rigor, competitor research depth, deliverable-ranking performance and exercise outputs. Mentors can set the cohort from those.

The same mentors everywhere

Teams outside Bucharest get the same mentors as Bucharest teams. The known gap is travel burden, which is still being worked out.

Recruiting

Pre-program and recruitment

The pre-program targets new candidate teams for the Hackathon, and teams that did not enter the previous edition's program. Those teams were accepted into the Hackathon, had at least one pitch and engaged with the jurors and the community.

The pre-program starts in October, in the app: an idea-validation journey with content, games and deliverables with mentor feedback, plus 1:1 mentoring for active teams.

WhatsApp community

For article sharing and for guiding teams through their first sets of deliverables. Both 2026 Hackathon participants and currently pre-registered teams and people are invited. It needs a named owner.

Industry deep dives

An expert talks about the real challenges of an industry, then participants brainstorm and ask questions to validate their ideas against it quickly. Partners talk publicly about the state of their industry, as a series teams can use.

Idea selection support

Help teams find viable niches. Unsexy industries that need automation get surfaced deliberately, as do boring businesses that get a foot in the door. B2C works for teams with a specific skillset or an existing community, and B2B works where IL can supply enough business insight.

AI / B2C Recruitment Bootcamp

The purpose is to help teams choose a problem and prototype a solution with AI. A stated alternative worth testing is a critical-thinking-when-building-with-AI session, which also tests the program's AI stance before anyone is committed. The bootcamp is also a live prototype for two mechanisms needed later: mentor assignment and mock-interview matching. It builds on the AI Validation Bootcamp line running since November 2025.

The Hackathon

Team clustering and the way back in

The Lean Model Canvas and experiment deliverable are replaced by articulating the problem you solve, plus competitive research. That prepares teams for jury questions better than the canvas did. Teams then cluster into four groups, each with its own two-week path.

Any traction

Teams with early signs of traction.

High potential overall

Teams strong on idea and people.

High potential team

Needs a pivot within 2 weeks.

High potential idea

Needs qualified people added to the team within 2 weeks.

Mentors can register interest in going hands-on with a specific team if it enters the program. Teams that do not enter get flagged as candidates for next year. Evaluation uses different criteria for B2C versus B2B and for software versus hardware, because a single rubric across all four is part of why hardware teams have been underserved.

The alumni return path

Getting IL26 people into IL27 is still open. Three pipelines have no defined route into the next edition yet: teams that did not make the program, teams that did and want another run, and alumni who could come back as coaches or hands-on mentors. The Hackathon flagging mechanism covers the first.
